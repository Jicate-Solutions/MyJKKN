1. What is PDE

The Principal Development Engine (PDE) is JKKN's post-AI higher-education engine. Its premise: AI now handles most content delivery (lectures, summaries, explanations, even feedback on routine work). The durable value a human institution can still produce is capability that AI cannot fake — exercised judgment, embodied skill, problem-finding, accountability, social leadership, cultural-civic grounding, and earned credentials.

PDE is the system that captures and validates those seven categories of capability across your students' time at JKKN. Instead of asking "did this learner consume the content?", it asks "what has this learner demonstrated, and who attests to it?"

Why this matters now

PDE gives JKKN three things at once: NAAC and NBA evidence that goes beyond attendance and marks; an employer signal that says what a graduate can actually do; and learner agency — every student carries a record of their own demonstrated capability, owned by them, not buried in a marks register.

You are not being asked to grade more. You are being asked to attest — to put your name on a learner's demonstration and confirm it was authentic. That attestation is what makes the record trustworthy.

2. The seven categories

Every demonstration in PDE falls under exactly one of seven categories. The categories were cross-validated against Fink's taxonomy, the OECD Learning Compass, and India's National Education Policy. Together they cover what a human professional must be able to do in a world where AI handles routine cognitive work.

Judgment judgment

The capacity to weigh trade-offs, sit with ambiguity, and decide under uncertainty. Example demonstrations: a clinical reasoning case where the learner explains their differential diagnosis, a design choice defended against alternatives, an ethics dilemma worked through in writing. Attest when: the learner has shown how they decided, not just what they decided.

Embodied skill embodied

Capability that lives in the hands and the body — clinical procedures, lab technique, surgical skill, prescription compounding, machine operation, physical assessment. Example demonstrations: OSCE-style station, supervised procedure, simulated patient encounter, lab protocol executed end-to-end. Attest when: you have watched the learner perform the skill or reviewed reliable evidence of performance (video, simulator log, signed supervisor checklist).

Problem-finding problem_finding

The capacity to notice a problem worth solving — not just solve a problem someone else has framed. Example demonstrations: a learner identifies a clinical workflow gap in their hospital posting and proposes an intervention; a project proposal framed around a real unmet need; a literature review that names a gap others have missed. Attest when: the problem the learner named was theirs to name — not assigned, not hinted at.

Accountability accountability

Following through on what you said you would do, including when it gets hard or unglamorous. Example demonstrations: a multi-week project shipped on its stated timeline; an error or miss owned and corrected in writing; a commitment to a peer or patient honoured. Attest when: you have evidence the learner did what they said they would, or owned the gap if they did not.

Social leadership social_leadership

The capacity to make a group work better than its individuals — coordinating, mentoring, mediating, advocating. Example demonstrations: peer mentoring across a semester, leading a team project to completion, chairing a committee responsibly, organising a community outreach. Attest when: the learner's role visibly improved what others were able to do.

Cultural and civic capacity cultural_civic

Grounded participation in the cultural and civic life around the learner — language, tradition, community, polity. NEP-aligned. Example demonstrations: demonstrated proficiency in an Indian language beyond mother-tongue, a sustained community project, attunement to local tradition shown through participation, a civic engagement (panchayat work, public-health drive, voter education). Attest when: the learner's engagement was sustained and substantive — not a one-off photo opportunity.

Credential credential

Formal certifications, licences, and credentials earned from a third party JKKN recognises. Example demonstrations: a passed pharmacy council exam, an industry certification (Cisco, AWS, NPTEL), a clinical licence, a published paper accepted in a recognised venue. Attest when: the credential is real, current, and the evidence (certificate, ID, publication link) checks out.

Substrate note

The three categories with the most variance in how they look across disciplines — embodied, social leadership, cultural/civic — currently carry structured rubrics (see Section 6). The other four are validated against narrative attestation today; rubrics may be added later as patterns emerge.

3. Your role as faculty

In PDE you are a validator, not a grader. Validators do one thing: they look at a learner's submitted demonstration and attest, in writing, whether it represents authentic capability.

What attestation actually means

When you validate a demonstration, you are saying three things at once:

  • Authenticity — the work or performance is the learner's own (not AI-generated, not copied, not someone else's).
  • Sufficiency — the evidence shown is enough to credibly demonstrate the capability claimed.
  • Category fit — the demonstration sits cleanly in the category the learner selected (and if not, you say so).

You are not being asked to assign a percentage, rank the learner against peers, or pass judgment on their overall worth. You are answering: did this happen, and was it real?

How this differs from grading

Grading reduces a learner to a number. Validation produces a record — a dated, named, evidenced statement that "on this date, this learner demonstrated this capability, attested by me." Records accumulate into a portfolio the learner owns for life. The Director receives the same record. NAAC sees the same record. An employer sees the same record. There is one source of truth, and your name is on it.

Pace-cap context

PDE is rolling out gradually. The first 60-day window onboards up to 30 coordinators across all eight institutions, by Director policy (see Section 8). You may be among the first 30, or you may join in a later wave. Either way, the load is deliberately small at the start so quality stays high while the patterns settle.

How much time this takes

A typical validation reads in 5 to 10 minutes. Most of that is reading the learner's evidence carefully, not filling in the form. The form itself is short. If you find yourself spending more than 15 minutes on a single demonstration, that is usually a sign the evidence is thin and you should ask for more — not that the form is heavy.
